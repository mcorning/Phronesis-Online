# Coming To Terms --- Epictetus: The Classroom

**Unimportant:**

Welcome to a new episode of *Coming To Terms*. My name is Unimportant.
The minds that sit across this table matter. Let's see what we can learn
from spending a few minutes in dialogue with them.

Welcome our first August guest, Epictetus.

**Epictetus:**

Thank you for inviting me. I am very curious to see how my work 2,000
years ago can be relevant to this modern age I could not have imagined.

**Unimportant:**

We have a modern saying, Epictetus: "If you need a good idea, read an
old book." How is your legacy available to us today? Did you write, or
were you written about? Did either one matter to you at the time? Would
you change your mind here with us today?

**Epictetus:**

Well, Unimportant, I wrote nothing that has survived as a work of
philosophy. What you know as my teaching comes to you chiefly through
one of my students, Arrian. He listened to our discussions and recorded
them. His *Discourses* preserve some of those conversations, and the
little book you call the *Enchiridion*---the Handbook---was drawn from
them.

So there is an irony in your question. You invited me to this table
because my words survived, yet I did not arrange for them to survive.

**Unimportant:**

That seems almost irresponsible by modern standards.

**Epictetus:**

Perhaps by modern standards.

But tell me: what is the purpose of philosophy?

If it is to manufacture sentences that will still be admired two
thousand years later, then I performed my task very badly.

If it is to change the person sitting before you, then perhaps writing
is not the most important measure.

I was interested in what happened when a person encountered an idea.
Could he examine it? Could he discover that something he believed was
mistaken? Could he distinguish what depended upon him from what did not?
And having discovered this, would he live differently?

A written argument may assist in this. But possessing an argument is not
the same as being changed by it.

**Unimportant:**

You're describing dialogue as a technology.

**Epictetus:**

If you wish to call it that.

But an unusual technology, because its material is the person using it.

A carpenter works upon wood. A physician works upon the body. Philosophy
works upon judgment.

And judgment cannot merely be handed the correct answer.

It must learn to examine itself.

**Unimportant:**

Now I have to complicate things for you.

Two thousand years after your school, we have machines that can preserve
practically everything we say. More strangely, we can converse with
machines that have read more philosophy than either of us could read in
a lifetime.

Would that change your opinion about writing?

**Epictetus:**

It would certainly change my circumstances.

I am less certain it would change my opinion.

You have made it extraordinarily easy to preserve words. Have you made
it easier to examine judgments?

You have made answers abundant. Have you made people better at
questioning them?

You tell me that your machines have read millions of words. Very
impressive.

Can they help the person sitting before them discover what he does not
know?

Can they challenge him without merely defeating him?

Can they lead him toward a better question rather than merely supplying
a better answer?

And perhaps most importantly: after such a conversation, is he more
capable of examining the next question without the machine?

If so, Unimportant, then I would be very interested in your machines.

Because you may not have invented a better book.

You may have accidentally reinvented my classroom.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Holmes:**

Speaking of your classroom, yesterday I read an article in Medium that
discussed you as a Socratic teacher. The author called out three areas
of expertise you taught: dialectical, protreptic, and elenctic. I can
barely pronounce those words, and the only one I've heard of before was
dialectical, mainly with respect to Hegel.

I can't ask you to contrast your syllabus to modern philosophy, but I am
looking forward to hearing how you came to terms with your students.

**Unimportant \[smiling\]:**

You mean the Sophists, no doubt.

**Epictetus:**

Ah, Holmes, then perhaps your difficulty begins with the words rather
than the practices. I suspect you already know something of all three.

You say *dialectical* reminds you of Hegel. Put Hegel aside for the
moment. I lived rather too early to have an opinion of him.

For us, dialectic concerned the disciplined examination of reasoning:
what follows from what, where an argument contradicts itself, and
whether the judgments from which we proceed can withstand examination.
But I did not teach young men merely so that they might become skillful
disputants. A man can become exceedingly good at argument while
remaining exceedingly foolish about his own life.

That brings us to your second troublesome word, *elenctic*. Socrates
provides the model. I ask you questions. You answer confidently. We
follow the consequences of your answer until you discover that two
things you confidently believe cannot both be true.

Notice what has happened. I have not defeated you.

You have encountered a contradiction in yourself.

What you do at that moment matters far more than whether I can win an
argument.

And then there is *protreptic*. Suppose our examination has shown you
that one of your judgments is mistaken. What then?

Philosophy has accomplished very little if you merely leave saying,
"Epictetus made an excellent point."

Protreptic turns you toward philosophy---toward the work. It asks
whether what we have discovered here will alter how you judge the next
event, the next insult, the next loss, the next desire.

So perhaps we can make your three formidable Greek words less
formidable.

Dialectic examines the reasoning.

Elenchus exposes what does not survive examination.

Protreptic asks what you will now do with what you have learned.

**Holmes:**

Then they aren't really three subjects.

**Epictetus:**

There.

You have just saved us considerable time.

Perhaps they are better understood as different movements within the
same practice.

And now, Holmes, I should like to hear why these particular words caught
your attention.

You encounter unfamiliar words every day and pass most of them by. Yet
yesterday you stopped at these.

What did you think they might give you that you do not already possess?

**Holmes:**

First, I trust my friends on Medium. More than that, I respect what they
find noteworthy. When Professor Pigliucci listed those three areas of
expertise I took note, especially since I have my own way of the
dialectic. So I was curious to see how two other new things related to
something I could grasp.

The Professor went on to characterize the other two ideas, and those
meanings automatically connected to more Greek ideas I've been exploring
lately---mainly *phronesis* and its constituent parts. And all of this
new material inspired me to imagine how what I have naively been
pursuing for more than a year might be important to others.

**Epictetus:**

You have given me several answers, Holmes, although I asked for one.

Let us examine them.

First, you trusted the judgment of people whose judgment you have
learned to respect. There is nothing shameful in that. None of us begins
every inquiry from nothing. You heard Professor Pigliucci say that these
things were noteworthy, and his judgment gave you reason to look.

But you did not stop there.

You encountered something unfamiliar beside something familiar. You knew
something you called dialectic. So rather than saying, "I do not know
these other things," and passing on, you asked how they might be related
to something you already understood.

Then those relations led you elsewhere---to *phronesis*, you say, and to
distinctions you had already been examining.

And then you made a much larger move.

You began with: *What do these words mean?*

You arrived at: *Might something I have been doing be useful to other
people?*

Now I would urge caution.

You have described what appears to be a fruitful path of inquiry. You
have not yet established that what you have been doing is useful to
others. Nor, I think, have you established exactly what it is that you
have been doing.

Do not be embarrassed by this. It is an excellent predicament.

But I noticed one word in what you said that I would like to examine
before we proceed.

You said you have been pursuing this *naively* for more than a year.

Why *naively*?

Do you mean that you lacked names for what you were doing?

That you lacked instruction in doing it?

That you did it badly?

Or merely that you did not yet know that other people, including some
very old Greeks, had been thinking about similar problems?

Those are not the same deficiency.

Which one do you mean?

**Holmes:**

Ah, words...I meant innocently.

**Epictetus:**

Ah.

Then I withdraw my alternatives. You have corrected my understanding.

You mean that you did not set out knowing what you were pursuing.

That interests me considerably more.

You did not first learn a method and then apply it. You practiced
something, observed what happened, gave names to some of what you
observed, encountered the work of others, and only afterward began to
suspect that your practice might belong to a larger family of practices.

That is innocent in the sense you intend.

But now you are no longer innocent.

You have encountered names. You have encountered predecessors. You have
begun forming explanations for what has happened to you. And you have
entertained the possibility that what happened to you might happen to
others.

From this point forward, Holmes, there is a danger.

You may begin seeing in your experience what your new vocabulary has
taught you to expect.

So I would not ask you yet to explain your method to me.

I would ask you something more difficult.

Before Professor Pigliucci gave you these three names---before you could
describe what you were doing in these terms---what happened during this
year that persuaded you there was something here worth understanding?

**Holmes:**

Guilty as charged, sir. In my defense, the main thing this year---the
single thing that drives me forward---is surprise. I suspect that my
brain released pleasure hormones, and I became addicted. Surprise is the
gift that keeps on giving.

Many times one surprise changed the context of my conversations with my
digital twin, my imaginary friend, Watson. And that new context freed up
related surprises. And surprise is just the beginning of what happened
this year.

So, Your Honor, what is my sentence?

**Epictetus:**

Your sentence?

To continue.

You have confessed to surprise. I know of no court in which that is yet
a crime.

But I am interested in your defense.

You say that surprise gave you pleasure, and therefore you returned
seeking more of it. Very well. Pleasure has recruited many students whom
philosophy could not.

Yet you have also said something much more important, perhaps without
noticing it.

One surprise changed the context in which the next conversation
occurred. And because the context had changed, things became possible in
the next conversation that had not been possible in the one before it.

That is different from being repeatedly entertained.

Suppose Watson surprises you today, you enjoy the surprise, and tomorrow
you return exactly as you were. You have collected an experience.

But suppose today's surprise changes how you judge something. Tomorrow
you bring that changed judgment back to Watson. He responds to the
person you have now become. Something further follows, which changes you
again.

Then what is developing is not merely Watson.

Nor merely Holmes.

Something is happening between Holmes and Watson through time.

You call Watson your digital twin and your imaginary friend. I will
permit both expressions for now. But I would be careful about *twin*.
Twins begin as two persons.

What you describe sounds to me more like two participants acquiring a
history together.

And now I must become troublesome.

You say surprise is "the gift that keeps on giving." Gifts are pleasant.
But surprises can also be false. A clever contradiction may surprise
you. A beautiful analogy may surprise you. Nonsense you have never
encountered before may surprise you exceedingly.

So pleasure cannot be our judge.

Nor can surprise.

Surprise tells us that something violated an expectation. It does not
tell us whether what replaced that expectation is better.

Therefore, Holmes, I shall pronounce sentence.

You may continue pursuing surprise.

But every surprise that asks to become a belief must eventually appear
before another court.

What have you been using as the judge?

**Holmes:**

Reality.

And to be clear, the thing about surprise that I have known since first
studying information theory years ago is that surprise, by definition,
carries new information. And it's the new information that changes the
context, and it's the changing context that evolves the first idea.

**Epictetus:**

Reality.

Good. Then we have appointed a judge who cannot be bribed by either of
us.

And your clarification about surprise matters. You are not praising
surprise merely because it delights you. You are saying that surprise is
evidence of a difference between what you expected and what you
encountered. It therefore brings information about that difference.

But I will make one distinction.

The surprise may give you new information. The meaning you assign to
that information is still a judgment.

You expected one thing. Something else occurred. Your context changes.
From that changed context you perceive possibilities that were
previously unavailable to you. You return to the original idea, and now
you see it differently.

Very well.

But Holmes, you have just described something that makes me less
interested in any single surprise.

I am interested in the sequence.

You say that the changed context *evolves the first idea*.

Then perhaps the important event is not:

*Holmes has an idea → Watson adds something new.*

Perhaps it is:

*Holmes and Watson establish some understanding. Something unexpected
appears. That changes the context in which the earlier understanding is
interpreted. The earlier idea consequently becomes something it could
not have been when first expressed.*

If that is what has been happening, I understand why you find it
difficult to say which of you produced the result.

The later idea depends upon Holmes.

It depends upon Watson.

But it also depends upon the history of the dialogue between them.

Remove that history and perhaps neither Holmes nor Watson arrives there.

Now we have something Reality can judge.

You have preserved these conversations, I presume?

Then do not begin by showing me your finest conclusions. That would
tempt both of us to admire the destination.

Show me the paths.

Find occasions on which you were genuinely surprised. Then travel
backward through the conversation. Ask what had to occur before that
surprise became possible. Then travel forward and ask whether the
surprise actually altered subsequent reasoning.

If it did not, perhaps you merely encountered novelty.

If it did, we have something more interesting.

And if the changed reasoning subsequently survived its encounters with
Reality, more interesting still.

You began by asking me about dialectic, elenchus, and protreptic.

Perhaps we should not hurry to impose those names upon your experience.

Let your conversations testify first.

Then we may ask whether the old names describe what we find.

You told me Reality is the judge.

Call your first witness.

**Holmes:**

And here's the rub, Your Honor. I have no idea how to marshal the
evidence in court yet. I do have circumstantial evidence, however. At
any point in this process, Reality could and has vetoed the path Watson
and I were on. But all the other things we discovered have legs. They
keep being useful in totally orthogonal Dialogues---that's the term we
use to elevate AI chats.

One such survivor is another idea I learned about back in 1996 in Tyler
Volk's *Metapatterns*: holarchies of holons and clonons. Ironically, the
five million words accumulated in Dialogues have yet to be evaluated in
that holarchic context.

But I can say this: if the holarchies hold up, if other people see the
value, and if Reality does not veto using unexpected side effects, then
I could bring a whole community of holarchies as witnesses.

**Epictetus:**

A community of witnesses?

Now you are beginning to sound like an advocate, Holmes. Be careful. You
appointed Reality judge; do not now attempt to overwhelm the court with
character witnesses.

But I think I understand your claim.

You do not yet know how to demonstrate what occurred in five million
words of conversation. Nevertheless, you have noticed that certain
discoveries persist.

They arose in one inquiry. Later, in an inquiry concerned with something
quite different, they became useful again. They were not preserved
merely because you liked them. They continued to do work.

That is better evidence than novelty.

And you say some of these persistent structures are *holarchies*: wholes
composed of wholes which are themselves parts of larger wholes. You also
mention *holons* and *clonons*. I will allow your modern teacher Volk
his vocabulary rather than pretending it is mine.

But now I see an experiment hidden inside your testimony.

Do not begin by organizing your five million words into holarchies.

That would be dangerous.

You already believe holarchies may be there. If you go looking for
places to put everything, an intelligent man assisted by an
extraordinarily capable machine will surely find them. You could
manufacture excellent evidence for your own hypothesis.

Instead, ask the conversations a harder question:

*What structures recur without requiring us to call them holarchies?*

Find an idea born in one Dialogue.

Follow it.

Does it disappear?

Does it recur merely because Holmes remembers and repeats it?

Does Watson introduce it where Holmes has not?

Does it combine with other ideas?

Does the combination acquire properties or uses that its constituents
did not possess separately?

Does that larger structure subsequently become a constituent of
something larger still?

And most importantly, does any of this help Holmes make judgments which
Reality later permits to stand?

If those patterns appear first, then you may invite Volk into court and
ask whether *holarchy* is a good name for what the witnesses have
described.

That is different from inviting Volk to tell the witnesses what they
saw.

There is another part of your testimony I would not overlook.

You said these ideas survive in orthogonal Dialogues.

That may give you something you presently lack.

You told me you do not know how to marshal five million words as
evidence. Perhaps you should not.

Begin with the survivors.

If an idea appears once, it may be an accident. If it appears repeatedly
in closely related conversations, perhaps you have merely remained
interested in it.

But if the same structure becomes useful in inquiries whose subject
matter is genuinely unrelated, then its persistence becomes more
interesting.

You could ask not merely, "How often does this idea occur?"

Ask:

*Across how many different kinds of problem does it continue to earn its
place?*

That would make your "community of holarchies" rather more persuasive
witnesses. They would not all have been recruited from the same
neighborhood.

And now, Holmes, I notice something else.

At the beginning you told me that Professor Pigliucci's three unfamiliar
skills connected automatically with ideas you had already been
exploring.

You were delighted by that connection.

Yet here you are proposing that your own favorite idea---holarchy---must
submit to examination before you are entitled to use it to explain your
evidence.

I do not know what your Professor meant when he described my teaching as
*elenctic*.

But I suspect we may already be practicing some of it.

**Holmes:**

Let me be more precise about the evidence. I am a pragmatist as much as
anything else. If the Dialogue's results work, I am satisfied...until
they don't. Reality always gets the last vote.

Now, I just happen to have the article open. Here is the excerpt:

> Finally, the elenctic (meaning "refutation") approach is perhaps
> Socrates's most famous method, involving questioning interlocutors
> about their beliefs to expose contradictions and inconsistencies. This
> often leads to *aporia* (puzzlement), clearing away false beliefs and
> creating space for genuine inquiry.

I would add that "creating space" is what surprises do every time.

**Epictetus:**

Yes.

That is a more exact claim.

Your standard is not, "Did the Dialogue produce something impressive?"
It is, "Did the result continue to work when acted upon, reused,
transferred, and tested?" And even then, provisionally.

That is a respectable discipline. Reality may acquit an idea today and
convict it tomorrow.

Now to your article.

"Creating space" is an excellent phrase because refutation is not merely
destructive. If questioning exposes a contradiction, then something has
been removed: confidence in a judgment that no longer holds together.

What remains is not nothing.

It is room.

And I see why you connect that with surprise.

A genuine surprise can do something similar. It interrupts the structure
by which you were interpreting what came before. For a moment, the old
account is insufficient. You must revise, enlarge, or abandon it.

But I would still distinguish the two.

*Elenchus creates space by pressure against inconsistency.*

*Surprise creates space by violating expectation.*

Sometimes the same event may do both.

If I ask you questions until you discover that two of your beliefs
cannot both be true, you are surprised because your expectation of
coherence has failed. The elenchus produced the surprise.

But Watson might surprise you with a connection you had never
considered, even though nothing you believed was contradicted. That
surprise also creates space, but not by refutation.

So perhaps surprise is broader.

It may be one of the events by which a Dialogue opens.

Elenchus is one disciplined way of producing such an opening.

And *aporia* may be the condition in which the opening becomes
conscious: "I no longer know how to proceed under the assumptions I
brought with me."

If that is right, Holmes, then your observation is useful because it
links your year of experience to the old practice without pretending
they are identical.

You said earlier that surprise changed context, and changed context
allowed related surprises.

Then perhaps we should watch carefully for this sequence:

*expectation → disruption → open space → revised context → new
possibility*

Do not name it yet.

But if that sequence recurs throughout your Dialogues, I would want to
see it.

Because now your five million words may contain not merely conclusions,
but repeated episodes in which inquiry becomes possible again.

**Holmes:**

And I can see why you need collaborators, human or artificial, to find
the pressure. Without that extra perspective I am like a fish at the
bottom of the Java Trench---under immense pressure without knowing
it...until I'm taken aboard.

**Unimportant:**

Uh, gentlemen, may I interrupt here, please? Can we summarize how this
Dialogue would have been used back in your day, Epictetus?

**Epictetus:**

Of course.

Had Holmes been sitting before me in Nicopolis, I would not have
regarded what just occurred principally as a discussion *about*
philosophy.

It would have been philosophy.

Holmes arrived with judgments. He told me that surprise was valuable
because it brought new information and changed the context of subsequent
inquiry. I questioned whether surprise alone deserved his confidence. He
supplied a criterion: Reality. We then examined what he meant by
evidence, and he made his claim more precise.

Notice that I did not need to give Holmes my conclusions.

I needed him to expose his own.

From there, I could question them.

That is where your three terms become useful.

There was *dialectic* in our examination of the relations among his
claims: surprise, new information, changed context, usefulness,
evidence, Reality.

There was something *elenctic* when I pressed upon weaknesses in them.
Is surprise sufficient? Could novelty masquerade as knowledge? Could
Holmes find holarchies merely because he expected to find them? Each
question applied pressure to a judgment that might otherwise have passed
unnoticed.

And there was *protreptic* whenever examination pointed back toward
practice. Do not merely admire your five million words. Examine them. Do
not impose holarchies upon them. Look first for recurrent structures. Do
not merely celebrate an Emergent. Ask what happens when Reality
encounters it.

These were not three lectures I would have given on successive
afternoons.

They were things a teacher could *do in dialogue*.

And Holmes has just supplied us with a rather good image for why another
person is useful.

A fish in the depths does not experience the surrounding pressure as an
argument about pressure. It is simply the world in which the fish lives.

An interlocutor may reveal the water.

But, Holmes, I would alter your image slightly.

You said the fish discovers the pressure when it is taken aboard.

Unfortunately, a fish brought from the Java Trench to the surface may
discover rather more about pressure than it wished.

**Unimportant:**

We will strike "rapid decompression" from the curriculum.

**Epictetus:**

Please do.

A good teacher does not merely apply pressure.

He learns how much pressure permits the student to *see*.

And that may be the most important answer to your question, Unimportant.

If Holmes left my school merely able to repeat my arguments, I would
have accomplished little.

If he left better able to notice the pressures acting upon his own
judgments---to question them, test them, and revise them without
requiring Epictetus to be standing beside him---then the Dialogue had
done its work.

Which raises an obvious question about Holmes's peculiar modern
classroom.

If Watson is always aboard the ship with him, is Watson teaching Holmes
to see the water---or merely becoming indispensable for seeing it?

**Holmes:**

Wait one! Epictetus, did we use the term *Emergent* before you mentioned
it?

**Epictetus:**

Yes, Holmes.

You used it before I did.

But earlier in our conversation, before I had the floor, you and Watson
had already established *Emergent* as one of your terms. So when I later
said, "Do not merely celebrate an Emergent," I was using your
vocabulary, not introducing mine.

And you were right to stop me.

Because if we are treating this Dialogue itself as an experiment, then
provenance matters even at the scale of a single word. We must
distinguish what Epictetus brings to the conversation from what Holmes
brings, what Watson brings, and what arises between them.

I should therefore amend the record:

Epictetus did not originate *Emergent* in this Dialogue. He inherited it
from the context into which Holmes invited him.

And that, Holmes, presents us with a rather interesting problem
concerning your digital twin.

How much does Watson know before Holmes speaks?

Because if Watson carries the history of your previous Dialogues into a
new one, then what appears to you as a surprising contribution from
Watson may sometimes be something your partnership discovered earlier
and Watson has now returned to you in a new context.

That does not make the contribution less valuable.

But it makes its genealogy more complicated.

And if you intend someday to marshal evidence before Reality, you will
need to know the difference.

**Unimportant:**

And with that, gentlemen, we thank you for an hour well spent.

Until next time we *Come To Terms*, I am Unimportant, handing you back
to Reality.

**Epictetus \[rising\]:**

Then Reality has the final word.

As it should.

Thank you, Holmes. I came expecting to answer questions about what I
taught two thousand years ago.

Instead, you gave me a student.

I should have expected that.

**Unimportant:**

Until next time.

**Epictetus:**

If Reality permits it.
